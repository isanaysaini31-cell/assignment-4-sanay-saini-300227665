Why this matters for the Fraser Valley before the next pandemic

The shutdown was costly, and it began with a warning that was never issued

The first COVID-19 shutdown showed how costly a late warning can be. In March 2020 alone, Canada's real GDP fell 7.5% (Statistics Canada, 2020), and the full year was a 5.4% contraction, the largest annual drop since quarterly data were first recorded in 1961 (CityNews 1130, 2021). The BC economy shrank 3.8% in 2020 (Statistics Canada, 2021). Economists at Queen's University calculated that the impact on Canada from March 2020 to February 2021 was over $170 billion, or about 8.3% of GDP (Cotton et al., 2020). Public spending followed: a CBC analysis found that Ottawa had paid out $240 billion in eight months, or about $952 million per day (CBC News, 2020).

The warning system was part of the problem. The Auditor General of Canada determined that the Public Health Agency of Canada was not as prepared as it could have been, and that its Global Public Health Intelligence Network did not issue an alert about the virus that became COVID-19 (Auditor General of Canada, 2021). The Auditor General also found that the agency's two main early warning tools did not work as intended (CBC News, 2021). As of September 2023, there had been at least 374,106 COVID-19 hospital admissions and 46,472 deaths in Canada (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2026).

The Fraser Valley felt it first-hand

The region was a pressure point many times over. Fraser Health announced outbreaks at Chilliwack General Hospital, where transmission was detected on a medicine unit (Fraser Health, 2021), and a few days later at Abbotsford Regional Hospital, when the health authority described the region as a hot spot (Global News, 2021). Omicron continued to affect care homes, such as Menno Hospital in Abbotsford and Eden Care Centre in Chilliwack (Fraser Health, 2022). A community outbreak also hit an Abbotsford poultry-processing plant in December 2020 (Georgia Straight, 2020).

There are many people at risk, and the number is growing. At the 2021 census, Abbotsford had a population of 153,875 and Chilliwack 98,161 (Fraser Valley Regional District, 2022), and Abbotsford is projected to grow to approximately 192,000 by 2030 (Fraser Valley Regional District, 2025). Of the 317,670 off-reserve residents of the regional district, 24,010 (7.6%) identify as Indigenous (Fraser Valley Regional District, n.d.), so any plan must be created in partnership with Indigenous people, not just for them.

The gap: the Valley's sewers are not even on the map

The BC Centre for Disease Control (BCCDC) runs BC's wastewater program, which samples wastewater treatment plants two to three times per week and reports results for SARS-CoV-2, influenza and RSV on a public dashboard (BC Centre for Disease Control, n.d.). The Abbotsford-Mission (JAMES) and Chilliwack plants are not on the list of participating plants on that page (BC Centre for Disease Control, n.d.). The program covers about 64% of British Columbians, which leaves roughly a third of the population outside it (Canadian Water Network, 2024). Part of the reason the BCCDC expanded to the North late was that it did not have the specialized equipment or trained staff it needed (Zeidler, 2023). In 2025, CBC reported that funding for the BC program had ended, and that the Ontario program had already been closed in July 2024 (Kulkarni, 2025).

The next threat could start in our own barns

The Fraser Valley has already given off a pandemic-like warning sign. In 2004, two poultry workers in the Valley were infected with H7N3, and in November 2024 a teenager in B.C. was the first person in Canada to catch H5N1 domestically (CBC Radio, 2024). An estimated 17 million birds were culled in the Abbotsford-Chilliwack area in 2004 (CBC News, 2022), and the Valley is home to approximately 80% of the province's poultry farms (CTV News, 2022). Since the current outbreak began in 2022, Abbotsford has had 100 commercial flocks hit by avian influenza (Graber, 2024), and Fraser Health's communicable disease team already monitors the employees of affected farms (Fraser Health, n.d.). In a region with this much animal-human contact, an emerging virus needs to be identified in people as early as possible.

Wastewater is the right core

Wastewater monitoring provides lead time. In Saskatoon, reported new cases lagged the sewage viral load by about seven days (Xie et al., 2022). New SARS-CoV-2 lineages were detected between one and sixteen weeks before clinical testing in samples from Toronto Pearson airport (Overton et al., 2024). A pilot in a seniors' residence in Peterborough, Ontario used building-level sewage data to detect outbreaks earlier (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2023), and a 2026 Ontario model assumed a gain of one to ten days of lead time when testing whether the approach is cost-effective (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2026). In late 2021, when the BC testing system was stretched thin, the wastewater dashboard was one of the more reliable ways to monitor community virus levels (Kulkarni, 2025).

It is also inexpensive per unit. Published cost studies in low- and middle-income countries ranged from $34 to $517 per sample (Mathematica, n.d.), or $0.07 to $0.13 per person per year in two catchments (PATH, n.d.). A cost model of a United States military pilot found that wastewater monitoring complements other monitoring approaches and is highly cost-effective compared with them (Sanjak et al., 2024). One equipment vendor claims in-house testing costs about $40, against $200 to $600 or more at a third-party lab (Hach, n.d.); that is a marketing figure, so actual savings may be lower.

How this translates to a $1 billion budget. Sampling is cheap, and spending a billion dollars on sampling alone would be wasteful. The money is only worth the cost if it also provides what was missing in 2020: the resources and training (a regional laboratory and trained personnel) to support a medical health officer who can respond to a warning within hours, and the materials and practice (supplies and drills) to act on it. That is how this project is designed.

The concept: a network from sewer to response

The Fraser Valley Early Warning Network (FVEWN) has four interconnected components. First, a sampling network of 25 sites: the JAMES plant that treats Abbotsford, Mission and Sumas, Washington (City of Abbotsford, n.d.), the Chilliwack plant on Wolfe Road (City of Chilliwack, n.d.), the smaller plants at Agassiz and Hope (Global News, 2023), and facility-level sites such as long-term care homes, hospitals, UFV campuses, shelters, poultry-processing plants and Abbotsford airport. Second, the UFV Fraser Valley Pathogen Laboratory, which does local testing and sequences samples for variants. Third, a data and alert platform that converts results into a public dashboard and a private alert line to Fraser Health. Finally, a surge-readiness hub, which stores supplies and conducts drills so that a warning leads to action.

UFV is the natural home. It has campuses and locations in Abbotsford, Chilliwack, Mission and Hope (University of the Fraser Valley, n.d.-a), serves approximately 15,000 students per year (University of the Fraser Valley, n.d.-b), and is legally mandated to serve the Fraser-Cascade, Chilliwack, Abbotsford and Mission school districts (University of the Fraser Valley, 2022). The students who work in the lab also staff the response drills, and become the region's trained surge workforce.

2. Scope

Scope is what the project will and will not do. The project delivers the eight deliverables listed above for the six municipalities of the Fraser Valley Regional District and the surrounding rural and First Nations communities (Fraser Valley Regional District, 2022). It is a surveillance and preparedness project. It is not a hospital build, a vaccine program or a substitute for the BCCDC; the laboratory will be integrated into the provincial system and will share data with it (BC Centre for Disease Control, n.d.).

In scope

  • Sampling at 25 sites and mobile units
  • Laboratory building, equipment, accreditation, sequencing
  • Data platform, dashboard and alert protocol with Fraser Health
  • Targeted monitoring for avian influenza exposure and congregate care
  • Supply hub, drills, training, community communication
  • Audit, evaluation and handover

Out of scope

  • New acute-care hospital beds
  • Vaccine or drug research and development
  • Changes to provincial or federal public health law
  • Border and quarantine policy (federal responsibility)
  • Treatment-plant upgrades beyond sampling access
  • Individual-level tracking of people

Work breakdown structure (top level)

1 Management and governance; 2 Sampling network; 3 Laboratory; 4 Data and alerts; 5 Facility and agricultural monitoring; 6 Surge hub; 7 Workforce; 8 Communication; 9 Close-out. During planning, each is divided into work packages, each with an owner, a budget and an acceptance test.

Scope control

Any change that affects cost or moves a milestone goes to a change control board made up of the project manager, a finance officer, the clinical champion and a community member. Changes under $250,000 and expected to take less than two weeks can be approved by the project manager; larger changes must be approved by the board, and anything involving the contingency must be approved by the sponsor. These thresholds are team estimates.

How the cost is estimated and controlled

During the proposal phase I use analogous and parametric estimates, drawing on published per-sample and program costs (Mathematica, n.d.; Sanjak et al., 2024). In planning, estimates become bottom-up, based on vendor quotations and design drawings. During execution the project tracks earned value, and any forecast overrun above 5% on a work package triggers a recovery plan. The $100 million contingency (10%) is held by the sponsor, not the project manager. Following the Project Management Institute (Project Management Institute, 2021), the baseline changes only through the change control board.

5. Three spheres of systems management

Schwalbe's systems view suggests that the manager should consider the business, organizational and technology spheres together (Schwalbe, 2019).

Business

The business question is value: a billion public dollars must deliver measurable protection and a plan for after 2029. The sustainment fund, the handover agreement and the ROI analysis answer it.

Organization

Several organizations with different cultures need to work together: a university, a health authority, municipal utilities, First Nations and farms. Governance, agreements and a steering committee make that possible.

Technology

PCR and sequencing laboratory methods, sampling equipment, secure data storage and a dashboard. Technology choices follow established laboratory practice (Association of Public Health Laboratories, 2026) and are linked with provincial data (BC Centre for Disease Control, n.d.).

7. Champion

A project this big needs a senior person who speaks up for it in rooms the project manager is not in. I propose two. The executive champion is a senior UFV officer, such as the Vice-President responsible for research, who protects the laboratory's place in the university's plans. The clinical champion is a Fraser Health medical health officer, who owns the alert pathway and vouches for the credibility of the data. Naming the actual people would be part of pre-initiation; I have not assumed anyone has agreed.

9. Project life cycle model

Schwalbe (Schwalbe, 2019) contrasts predictive development cycles (waterfall) with iterative ones (spiral and agile). For this project I chose a predictive, stage-gated waterfall, with iterative sprints within the data platform. Waterfall fits because the scope is well known, the laboratory build has a strict sequence (design, construction, commissioning, accreditation), and public funds require a formal gate before each tranche is released. In a pure spiral model the budget would be hidden in repeated prototypes. The data platform is different: dashboards and alert rules improve with user feedback from Fraser Health and the public, so they are developed in two-month sprints between the gates.

Before the project is formally approved, the team carries out the work that decides whether it should go ahead. These tasks run from late November 2026 to January 2027.

  • Confirm the business need using the evidence in this proposal and a short needs assessment with Fraser Health and the BCCDC.

  • Prepare the charter for approval.

12. Progress reports

The project reports on a regular schedule so that bad news travels quickly. Each month the project manager issues a status report to everyone involved, including percent complete, earned value indices (schedule and cost performance), top risks, top issues and decisions required. Each quarter the steering committee receives a gate review rated on scope, time, cost, quality and risk using a traffic-light system. Each tranche of funding is released only after the quarterly review. A public progress page repeats the headline measures: sites online, laboratory milestones and dashboard availability.

14. Return on investment and payback

Public health does not make money; the return is the losses avoided. Canada's shutdown cost over $170 billion in a year (Cotton et al., 2020), or about $14.2 billion per month. The Valley's off-reserve population is approximately 318,000 (Fraser Valley Regional District, n.d.), about 0.9% of Canada's population (team estimate, using a national population of about 37 million). Applying that share gives roughly $120 million of regional output lost for each month of pandemic-scale restrictions. This is a rough, proportional estimate, and I treat it only as an order of magnitude.

ROI is calculated as (benefit minus cost) divided by cost. Under these assumptions the project breaks even if it helps avoid roughly eight to nine months of pandemic-scale restrictions in the Valley. That is a high bar for one event, so I do not claim the money pays for itself through COVID-style savings alone. The case rests on three further points. The network can detect many kinds of threats (respiratory viruses, enteric disease and avian influenza), since these show up in wastewater (Kulkarni, 2025; Fraser Health, n.d.). Warnings can shorten restrictions rather than prevent them entirely, because decisions can be targeted at specific sites such as poultry plants and care homes. The benefits also include lives and hospital capacity, which are not captured in GDP figures: by September 2023 Canada had 64,623 ICU admissions (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2026). The project's operating cost after 2029 is covered by the $60 million sustainment fund and the handover agreement, so the payback period is judged against avoided loss, not revenue.

15. Project quality management

Quality here has two dimensions: the project is delivered correctly, and the product (the laboratory result) is reliable. Both use the PMBOK approach of planning quality, managing quality through process audits, and controlling quality through inspection and testing (Project Management Institute, 2021). Laboratory quality is built on personnel competency, a contamination-control layout and a documented quality management system (Association of Public Health Laboratories, 2026). Results are checked against the provincial program before the dashboard is released, because a false alarm leads to a loss of trust and a missed signal defeats the point of the project.

17. Testing and the cost of quality

Testing is carried out at every level. In the laboratory: negative and positive controls in each run, recovery tests using known virus added to samples, and regular blind proficiency samples exchanged with the BCCDC. In the platform: unit and acceptance tests for each sprint, penetration testing before launch and a data-reconciliation check against manual entry. In the response chain: a tabletop exercise and two live drills with Fraser Health, in which a simulated signal is followed from the sewer to a public notice. Construction is accepted only after commissioning tests are passed.

The cost of quality has four components. Money spent on prevention (training, design reviews, standard procedures) and appraisal (inspection, proficiency testing, audits) is spent to get it right. Internal failure (repeating runs, rejecting samples) and external failure (a missed or false alert reaching the public) are the cost of getting it wrong. The plan focuses on prevention and appraisal because external failure is by far the most costly. I budget around 8% of laboratory and platform spending for prevention and appraisal (team estimate).

19. Motivating the team

Schwalbe summarizes motivation theories including Maslow's hierarchy, Herzberg's motivation-hygiene theory and McClelland's needs (Schwalbe, 2019). Herzberg's distinction is the most useful here. Hygiene factors, such as fair pay, safe laboratories, reasonable hours and clear supervision, prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate anyone. Motivators, such as achievement, recognition, responsibility and a visible purpose, do. The project provides both: market-based salaries and protected shifts, and a steady stream of recognition built into the work, such as sharing the weeks when the network caught something early, naming staff on the public dashboard, funding conference presentations and giving real responsibility to junior staff and students. Because much of the work is routine sampling and testing, rotating people between field, laboratory and analysis roles helps prevent boredom.

20. Resource planning

Resources include people, but also space, equipment, vehicles, software and money. The longest-lead item is the laboratory site, which needs space in a UFV facility (Abbotsford is the working assumption) with clean and dirty areas separated (Association of Public Health Laboratories, 2026). Equipment is staged in two waves so that spending follows readiness. People are phased according to the Gantt chart: the project office first, then laboratory staff, then field technicians as each sampler wave is commissioned. A resource calendar, updated monthly, flags where two work packages compete for the same resources, most likely in Q3 and Q4 2028 when commissioning, the second sampler wave and platform sprints overlap.

Controlling risks

Each risk has an owner and a trigger. The project manager checks the register monthly, the steering committee checks the top five quarterly, and any risk that reaches an impact of 5 is escalated to the sponsor. Responses are to avoid, mitigate, transfer (through contract terms or insurance) or accept, with the contingency reserve applied to accepted and residual risks (Project Management Institute, 2021).

24. Handling stakeholders

High-influence, high-interest groups (the funder, BCCDC, Fraser Health, UFV leadership and First Nations) are managed closely, with a seat on the steering committee. High-interest groups with lower influence, such as care home operators, poultry producers and residents, are kept informed and given a formal route to raise concerns. Medium groups, such as vendors, are monitored. Four tactics apply throughout: engage early, ask each group what success means to them, never let a partner learn bad news from the media, and record commitments in the stakeholder register. The poultry sector needs special care because early detection must not feel like punishment; the agreement with producers states that signals are used for protection and support (Graber, 2024).

25. Closing process

Closing starts in Q3 2029, not in December, because the fixed end date of December 31, 2029 leaves no room for a late start. It has four tracks.

  • Administrative closure. Verify that all deliverables are accepted as required, get sign-off from the sponsor and both champions, and keep records.
  • Contract closure. Check deliveries, settle claims, complete final accounts and release holdbacks on all contracts.
  • Handover. Transfer the laboratory, platform and hub to the long-term operator under the operating agreement, supported by the $60 million sustainment fund, and move staff to permanent roles or release them with references and notice.
  • Learning and accountability. Hold a review with partners, complete an independent audit, return any remaining contingency to the funder, and release a final report and a public summary.

The project is closed when the sponsor signs the final acceptance. A post-implementation review is planned for twelve months later to check whether the benefits, such as signal lead time and alert speed, are being achieved.
